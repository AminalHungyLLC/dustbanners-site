Proving Ground

How Dustbanners gets balanced before anyone plays it.

Before people sit down at a table, the computer plays Dustbanners against itself, over and over. Every game is written down: which banner won, how it won, who went first, and how many rounds it took.

Those records show where the game leans one way. When a card or a rule tips the balance, it gets changed and tested again, so the game is fair before it reaches players. The games run on Kyle’s laptop and on computers in the cloud.

Who played these gamesThese are computer players, not people. The numbers below speak to balance only. They say nothing about how fun a game is or how long it takes at a real table.

The big counts

17,000+

More than 17,000 simulated games played so far, across many test versions of the game.

7 in 10

About 7 in 10 of those games ran in the cloud. About 3 in 10 ran on Kyle’s laptop.

2,000

Games that tried out possible changes: 14 rule options, one card change and one setting for the computer player.

120

Games played so far under the current rules. All 120 finished with a winner: none stalled, none crashed.

The current rules

  • Hold 8 of the 9 places at the end of a turn to win.
  • Breaking the other side’s Leader still wins too.
  • The second player draws 1 extra card in the opening hand.
  • The first player gets 1 less Supply on turn 1.

Where the numbers come fromEvery result on this page comes from 120 games under these rules: 12 banner pairings, 10 games each, computer against computer.

How a rule gets decided

The rule for winning by holding places changed recently. Here is how that happened, step by step.

  1. Spot the lean. Under the old rule, which needed one place fewer, most games ended by holding places, and the player who went first won more often than the player who went second.
  2. Try the options on the same games. 14 rule options were played on the same starting setups, so a difference in the results comes from the rule, not from the shuffle.
  3. Compare against the targets. Holding should win a little over half the games (between 50% and 60%), and the first player should win between 45% and 55%. Only one of the 14 options landed inside both targets in those games: holding 8 of the 9 places, together with the extra opening card for the second player and 1 less Supply for the first player on turn 1. The other rule options missed one target or both.
  4. Kyle makes the call. The results go to Kyle, who decides. He chose that option, and it is now the rule.
  5. Keep checking. 120 games is a first look, not the last word. Testing goes on as the game grows.

What these numbers are not

  • They come from the computer playing itself, not from people.
  • They do not measure fun, or how long a game takes at a real table.
  • They are small samples. A share near 50% from 120 games can be off by about 9 points either way by chance.
