Proving Ground

How Dustbanners is being tested before anyone plays it.

As of October 16, 2026. These counts come from games played by an older test version. Our version numbers were reset since then, so 0.6 is newer than 4.0. Version 0.6 is in testing now, and new counts will come later. Every number here is a current setting, not a final one.

Before people sit down at a table, the computer plays Dustbanners against itself, over and over. Every game is written down: which Banner won, how it won, who went first, and how many rounds it took. The first box has two factions, the Wardens and the Raiders. In the game, each faction is a Banner. More factions are to come.

Those records show where the game leans one way. When a card or a rule tips the balance, it gets changed and tested again. The games run on Kyle’s laptop and on computers in the cloud.

Who played these gamesThese are computer players, not people. The numbers below speak to balance only. They say nothing about how fun a game is or how long it takes at a real table.

120

Games played in these rule trials, under the older rules (hold 8 of the 9). All 120 finished with a winner: none stalled, none crashed.

The current rules

  • Hold all 9 places at the end of a turn to win. That is the rule today.
  • Breaking the other side’s Leader still wins too.
  • Each player draws 5 opening cards.
  • Both players start their first turn with 3 Supply.

The results below come from the older rules: hold 8 of the 9, the second player drew 1 extra opening card, and the first player got 1 less Supply on turn 1. They are not results for today's rules.

Where the numbers come fromEvery result on this page comes from 120 games under those older rules: 12 Banner pairings, 10 games each, computer against computer.

How a rule gets decided

The rule for winning by holding places has changed more than once. Here is how the change from hold 7 to hold 8 happened, step by step.

  1. Spot the lean. Under the hold-7 rule, which needed one place fewer than hold 8, most games ended by holding places, and the player who went first won more often than the player who went second.
  2. Try the options on the same games. 14 rule options were played on the same starting setups, so a difference in the results comes from the rule, not from the shuffle.
  3. Compare against the targets. Holding should win a little over half the games (between 50% and 60%), and the first player should win between 45% and 55%. Only one of the 14 options landed inside both targets in those games: holding 8 of the 9 places, together with the extra opening card for the second player and 1 less Supply for the first player on turn 1. The other rule options missed one target or both.
  4. Kyle makes the call. The results go to Kyle, who decides. He chose that option then. Later he changed the win to holding all 9 places, with the same opening for both players.
  5. Keep checking. 120 games is a first look, not the last word. Testing goes on as the game grows.

What these numbers are not

  • They come from the computer playing itself, not from people.
  • They do not measure fun, or how long a game takes at a real table.
  • They are small samples. A share near 50% from 120 games can be off by about 9 points either way by chance.
  • They are current settings, not final ones. Rules and card numbers can still change, and so can these results.
